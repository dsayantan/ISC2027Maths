Q1 (i)Objective[1]Matrices
If A and B are symmetric matrices of the same order, then (AB′ − BA′) is a:
  • (a)skew symmetric matrix
  • (b)null matrix
  • (c)symmetric matrix
  • (d)none of these
  1. A and B symmetric means A′ = A and B′ = B, so AB′ − BA′ = AB − BA.
  2. Transpose it: (AB − BA)′ = (AB)′ − (BA)′ = B′A′ − A′B′ = BA − AB.
  3. That is −(AB − BA).

Note So the matrix equals the negative of its own transpose — the definition of skew symmetric. It is the null matrix only in the special case AB = BA, which is not given.

Trap (AB)′ = B′A′, with the order reversed. Keeping the order gives the wrong sign and the wrong answer.

Answer a skew symmetric matrix

Worked solution shared with the chapter-ordered First Term Revision Paper, so the two cannot disagree.

Q1 (x)Objective[1]Applications of Derivatives
Assertion (A): f(x) = x3 − 3x2 + 6x − 100 is strictly increasing on ℝ − {1}.
Reason (R): a strictly increasing function is an injective function.
  • (a)Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
  • (b)Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
  • (c)Both A and R are false
  • (d)A is true but R is false

Note The paper's wording is loose, and the loose bit is worth naming. ℝ − {1} is not an interval, and ‘increasing’ is defined on intervals. What is true, and stronger, is that f is strictly increasing on the whole of ℝ.

  1. f ′(x) = 3x2 − 6x + 6 = 3(x2 − 2x + 2) = 3[(x − 1)2 + 1].
  2. That is at least 3 for every real x, so f ′ > 0 everywhere and f is strictly increasing on ℝ — which certainly gives A, since removing a point cannot spoil it.
  3. R is a true statement in its own right: strictly increasing ⇒ one-one.
  4. But R does not explain A. A is about the sign of the derivative; R is a consequence of monotonicity, not a reason for it.

Trap The examiner's intended trap is the deleted point: candidates assume f ′ must vanish at x = 1 for the paper to have excluded it. It does not — f ′(1) = 3.

Answer Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A

Q8Subjective[4]Relations and Functions
Test whether the relation R on ℤ defined by R = {(a, b) : |a − b| ≤ 5} is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
  1. Reflexive. |a − a| = 0 ≤ 5 for every integer a, so (a, a) ∈ R. R is reflexive.
  2. Symmetric. |a − b| = |b − a|, so (a, b) ∈ R forces (b, a) ∈ R. R is symmetric.
  3. Transitive. Take a = 1, b = 6, c = 11. Then |1 − 6| = 5 ≤ 5 and |6 − 11| = 5 ≤ 5, so (1,6) and (6,11) lie in R.
  4. But |1 − 11| = 10 > 5, so (1, 11) ≠ R. R is not transitive.

Note One counter-example is enough to kill transitivity, and it must be produced explicitly — saying ‘the gaps can add up’ earns nothing.

Trap A bounded-difference relation is never transitive: differences add, but the bound does not.

Answer Reflexive and symmetric, but not transitive

Q9Subjective[4]Continuity and Differentiability
If (ax + b) eyx = x, prove that x3 d2ydx2 = [x dydx − y]2.

Note Take logarithms first. Differentiating the equation as it stands means the product and chain rules on an exponential of a quotient; taking logs turns it into three easy terms.

  1. eyx = xax + b, so yx = log x − log(ax + b) and
    y = x log x − x log(ax + b)
  2. First derivative. dydx = log x + 1 − log(ax + b) − axax + b.
  3. The bracket. x dydx − y collapses beautifully — every logarithm cancels:
    x dydx − y = x − ax2ax + b = bxax + b
  4. so the right-hand side is bx2(ax + b)2.
  5. Second derivative. Differentiating dydx again,
    d2ydx2 = 1x − 2aax + b + a2x(ax + b)2
  6. Multiply by x3 and put everything over (ax + b)2:
    x3 d2ydx2 = x2[(ax + b) − ax]2(ax + b)2 = b2x2(ax + b)2

Q6 (b)Subjective[5]Application of Integrals
Find the area enclosed by the curve y2 = 2x − x2 and the straight line y = x.

Note Recognise the curve first: y2 = 2x − x2 rearranges to (x − 1)2 + y2 = 1, a circle of radius 1 centred at (1, 0).

  1. Intersections. Substituting y = x: x2 = 2x − x2 gives 2x2 − 2x = 0, so x = 0 or x = 1 — the points (0, 0) and (1, 1).
  2. (1, 1) is the top of the circle and (0, 0) is its left-hand end, so the line cuts off the piece above it.
  3. Area between them, upper boundary the circle y = √1 − (x − 1)2, lower boundary the line:
    A = ∫10[√1 − (x − 1)2 − x] dx
  4. Put u = x − 1 in the first part: ∫0−1√1 − u2 du is a quarter circle of radius 1, area π4.
  5. The second part is ∫10x dx = 12.
  6. A = π4 − 12.

Note Sanity check: π4 − 12 ≈ 0·285, a plausible sliver between a unit circle and a chord of it.

Answer Area = π4 − 12 ≈ 0·285 square units

Q13 (b)Subjective[5]Linear Programming
Every gram of wheat provides 0·1 g of protein and 0·25 g of carbohydrates; every gram of rice provides 0·05 g of protein and 0·5 g of carbohydrates. Wheat costs ₹5 per kg and rice ₹20 per kg. The minimum daily requirements are 50 g of protein and 200 g of carbohydrates. In what quantities should they be mixed to meet the requirements at least cost?

Note The same problem as Q22 of the 2024–25 First Term paper, with the two grains exchanged and the prices changed. Working both is the best test of whether you can formulate rather than recall.

  1. Let the diet contain x g of wheat and y g of rice.
    Protein: 0·1x + 0·05y ≥ 50 ⇒ 2x + y ≥ 1000
    Carbohydrate: 0·25x + 0·5y ≥ 200 ⇒ x + 2y ≥ 800
  2. Cost per gram is ₹0·005 for wheat and ₹0·02 for rice, so minimise C = 0·005x + 0·02y.
  3. Corner points of the unbounded region: (0, 1000), (400, 200) and (800, 0).
  4. C(0, 1000) = ₹20; C(400, 200) = 2 + 4 = ₹6; C(800, 0) = ₹4.
  5. Unbounded check. The smallest is 4; and 0·005x + 0·02y < 4 has no point in common with the region, so 4 is genuinely the minimum.

Note The answer is all wheat — 800 g of it, no rice — because wheat is four times cheaper per gram and carries twice the protein. Check the requirements: protein 80 g ≥ 50 ✓, carbohydrate 200 g ≥ 200 ✓, the second binding exactly.

Trap With the First Term paper's numbers the optimum is an interior corner; here it slides onto an axis. Never assume the answer is the intersection of the two constraint lines — test every corner.

Answer 800 g of wheat and no rice, at a minimum cost of ₹4
