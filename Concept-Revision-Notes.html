1. Relations and Functions

Four questions, and between them they test every definition in the chapter. Nothing here needs calculation — it is all about stating a definition precisely and then checking it.

1.1The three properties, with their quantifiers

Reflexive: (a, a) ∈ R for every a in the set. Symmetric: whenever (a, b) ∈ R, also (b, a) ∈ R. Transitive: whenever (a, b) and (b, c) are both in R, so is (a, c).

Watch outReflexive needs every element, so one missing pair kills it. Symmetric and transitive are conditional — they only ask for something when the premise holds.

Needed forFT Q1(xv), FT Q8

1.2Vacuous truth

If a relation contains no pair (a, b) and (b, c) to trigger transitivity, the condition is satisfied vacuously and the relation is transitive.

Watch outThis is the commonest one-mark trap in the chapter. R = {(1, 2)} is transitive; R = {(2, 2)} is symmetric and transitive.

Needed forFT Q1(xv), CISCE competency Q28

1.3Equivalence relation and its classes

All three properties together. The classes partition the set, and two elements are in the same class exactly when they are related.

Watch outA parity condition is transitive; a bounded-difference condition such as |a − b| ≤ 5 is not — differences add but the bound does not.

Needed forFT Q8, NCERT Ch 1 (|a − b| even)

1.4One-one, onto, bijection — how each is proved

One-one: assume f(x1) = f(x2) and derive x1 = x2. Onto: take any y in the co-domain and solve y = f(x) for an x in the domain.

Needed forFT Q1(vi), Exemplar Ch 1 Q21(iii)

1.5Strictly increasing ⇒ one-one

If f is strictly increasing (or strictly decreasing) on an interval it is automatically one-one there. With continuity, the intermediate value theorem then makes it onto its range.

Watch outx|x| is strictly increasing across 0 because the modulus keeps the sign; x2 is not.

Needed forFT Q1(vi)

1.6Range against co-domain

The co-domain is where the function is declared to land; the range is what it actually reaches. A question asking for the range wants the second.

Needed forFT Q1(ii), Exemplar Ch 1 Q14

1.7Finding a range by completing the square

f(x) = x2 − 4x + 5 = (x − 2)2 + 1. On a restricted domain, read off what the bracket can do and push it through.

Watch outThe restriction to [2, ∞) is what makes the function one-one; without it the same formula is many-one.

Needed forFT Q1(ii)

2. Inverse Trigonometric Functions

Five questions. Every one of them turns on the principal value branch — the algebra is easy, and the marks are in knowing when an identity is allowed.

2.1The principal value branches

FunctionBranch
sin−1 x[−π2, π2]
cos−1 x[0, π]
tan−1 x(−π2, π2)
cot−1 x(0, π)

Watch outNothing else in this chapter matters as much. Learn the four.

Needed forFT Q1(iii), FT Q7, PB Q3(b), Exemplar Ch 2 Q5

2.2The two complementary identities

sin−1x + cos−1x = π2,    tan−1x + cot−1x = π2

Watch outWhenever two different inverse functions of the same x appear, reach for these first — they usually collapse the whole expression.

Needed forFT Q1(iii), FT Q3, Exemplar Ch 2 Q22, Exemplar Ch 2 Q26

2.3Folding an angle back into its branch

f−1(f(θ)) = θ only when θ is in the branch. Outside it, subtract the right period: π for tan−1, 2π for sin−1 and cos−1.

Watch outUsing 2π on a tangent is the usual error. The answer to FT Q7 is 9π4 − x, not π4 − x, purely because of the interval given.

Needed forFT Q7, Exemplar Ch 2 Q5

2.4Converting one inverse function into another

Draw the right triangle. sin−145 has opposite 4 and hypotenuse 5, so adjacent 3 and the angle is also tan−143.

Watch outOnly valid for the acute case; check the sign of the cosine before assuming it.

Needed forPB Q1(b), Exemplar Ch 2 Q14

2.5The tan−1 addition formula, and its condition

tan−1x + tan−1y = tan−1 x + y1 − xy,   xy < 1

Watch outWhen xy > 1 a ±π must be added — that is what the condition is protecting you from.

Needed forPB Q1(b), NCERT Ch 2

2.6Naming the angles, then expanding

For sin−1A ± sin−1B, set A = sinα, B = sinβ, read cosα and cosβ off the triangles, and expand sin(α ± β).

Watch outConfirm the sign of cos(α ± β) before quoting the answer as an inverse sine — the obtuse angle has the same sine.

Needed forPB Q3(b), Exemplar Ch 2 Q14

2.7Compressing a sin x + b cos x

sin x + cos x√2 = sin(x + π4) = cos(π4 − x). In general a sin x + b cos x = √a² + b² sin(x + φ).

Needed forFT Q7, Exemplar Ch 5 Q37

2.8Test every candidate root

Squaring or cross-multiplying inside an inverse-trig equation can manufacture roots that the branches then reject.

Watch outIn PB Q3(b) the algebra gives x = 2 and x = −1; only x = 2 survives.

Needed forPB Q3(b), Exemplar Ch 2 Q7

3. Matrices

Four questions: one on transposes, one decomposition, and two systems of equations solved by the inverse.

3.1Transpose rules

(A′)′ = A, (A + B)′ = A′ + B′, and — the one that matters — (AB)′ = B′A′, with the order reversed.

Watch outKeeping the order gives the wrong sign and therefore the wrong answer.

Needed forFT Q1(i), Exemplar Ch 3 Q63

3.2Symmetric and skew symmetric

A′ = A is symmetric; A′ = −A is skew. A skew matrix always has a zero diagonal, because aii = −aii.

Watch outThe zero diagonal is the quickest check that an answer is right.

Needed forFT Q1(i), PB Q1(a)

3.3The unique split

A = 12(A + A′) + 12(A − A′)

Watch outFirst bracket symmetric, second skew; the decomposition exists for every square matrix and is unique.

Needed forPB Q1(a), NCERT Ch 3 Ex 3.3, Exemplar Ch 3 Q52

3.4Multiplication does not commute

AB ≠ BA in general, which is why (A + B)2 = A2 + 2AB + B2 holds only when AB = BA.

Needed forFT Q1(i)

3.5Inverse by the adjoint

A−1 = 1|A| adj A,   |A| ≠ 0

Watch outadj A is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. Forgetting the transpose is the single commonest way to lose these marks.

Needed forPB Q2(b), FT Q11

3.6Solving a system by the matrix method

Write AX = B, check |A| ≠ 0, then X = A−1B. A unique solution exists precisely when |A| ≠ 0.

Watch outAlways substitute the answer back into all three equations — it costs ten seconds and catches every arithmetic slip.

Needed forFT Q11, PB Q2(b), Exemplar Ch 4 Q19, NCERT Ch 4

3.7Turning a word problem into a system

One variable per unknown price or quantity, one equation per purchase or condition. The matrix work is then mechanical.

Needed forFT Q11, NCERT Ch 4 (onion, wheat and rice)

4. Determinants

Four questions, none of which should be expanded by brute force. Every one is a properties question.

4.1The properties worth knowing by name

  • A common factor in a row or column comes out.
  • Two identical — or proportional — rows or columns make it zero.
  • Ri → Ri + kRj leaves the value unchanged.
  • Interchanging two rows changes the sign.

Needed forFT Q1(iv), FT Q6, PB Q2(a), Exemplar Ch 4 Q6, Q7, Q13

4.2The standard opening move

R1 → R1 + R2 + R3 (or the same with columns) often makes a whole row constant. Take that factor out, then clear the row with two more operations.

Watch outTwo operations are usually needed after the factor comes out; stopping after one leaves a determinant you still have to expand.

Needed forPB Q2(a), FT Q1(iv), Exemplar Ch 4 Q13

4.3Determinants of products and powers

|AB| = |A||B|,   |An| = |A|n,   |kA| = kn|A| for an n × n matrix

Watch out|kA| = kn|A|: the exponent is the order, not 1.

Needed forFT Q4

4.4Adjoint identities

A (adj A) = |A| In,   |adj A| = |A|n − 1

Needed forFT Q4 (and the family it belongs to)

4.5Checking a determinant identity cheaply

Substitute convenient numbers — a = b = c = 1, or x = 0 — into both sides. It will not prove the identity but it catches a wrong constant at once.

Needed forFT Q1(iv), PB Q2(a)

4.6Spotting a linear relation between columns

If one column is a combination of the others the determinant is zero. Look for it before expanding: (p + q)2 − (p − q)2 = 4pq is what makes FT Q6 vanish.

Needed forFT Q6, Exemplar Ch 4 Q7

5. Continuity and Differentiability

The heaviest chapter in both papers — eight questions. Half of them are ‘prove this second-order identity’, which is one template applied four times.

5.1Continuity at a point

limx→a−f(x) = limx→a+f(x) = f(a)

Watch outAll three parts, not two: the limits can agree with each other and still differ from the value.

Needed forFT Q1(ix), FT Q12, Exemplar Ch 5 Q11, Q16

5.2Differentiability implies continuity

A function differentiable at a point is continuous there. The converse is false — |x| at 0 is the standard counter-example.

Watch outSo a ‘derivable’ question carries two conditions: match the values and match the one-sided slopes.

Needed forFT Q12, CISCE competency Q22

5.3Piecewise functions: two conditions

At the join, continuity gives one equation and equality of the one-sided derivatives gives another. Two unknowns need both.

Watch outMatching only the slopes leaves the constant free and the function able to jump.

Needed forFT Q12, Exemplar Ch 5 Q16

5.4Parametric first derivative

dydx = dydtdxdt

Needed forFT Q1(vii), FT Q1(viii), Exemplar Ch 6 Q44

5.5Parametric second derivative — the trap

d2ydx2 = ddx(dydx) = ddt(dydx)dxdt

Watch outIt is not d2ydt2 ÷ d2xdt2. FT Q1(viii) is built so that the wrong rule gives the assertion's wrong answer — both halves of it are false.

Needed forFT Q1(viii)

5.6Simplify before you differentiate

elog t = t for t > 0, so y = elog sin−1 x + elog cos−1 x is just sin−1x + cos−1x = π2.

Watch outA constant differentiates to 0. Grinding through the chain rule gets there too, but slowly and with two chances to slip.

Needed forFT Q2, Exemplar Ch 5 Q37

5.7Logarithmic differentiation

Take logs of both sides first when the unknown appears in an exponent or as a product of many factors.

Watch out(ax + b)eyx = x becomes y = x log x − x log(ax + b) in one line.

Needed forFT Q9

5.8The second-order identity template

  1. Differentiate once and rearrange so the awkward root sits alone.
  2. Square both sides to clear it.
  3. Differentiate again.
  4. Divide through by 2dydx, noting it is not identically zero.

Watch outThis one template answers every ‘show that (1 ± x2)y″ ± xy′ = …’ question in either paper.

Needed forFT Q9(OR), PB Q5(a), Exemplar Ch 5 Q81

5.9Standard limits and small-angle expansions

limx→0sin xx = 1,   sin x = x − x36 + …,   cos x = 1 − x22 + …

Watch outTwo terms of each series settle a 00 limit faster than three applications of L'Hôpital.

Needed forPB Q1(d)

5.10Derivatives of the inverse trigonometric functions

ddx sin−1x = 1√1 − x2,  ddx tan−1x = 11 + x2

Needed forFT Q2, Exemplar Ch 5 Q37

6. Applications of Derivatives

Eight questions: two rates, three tangent-and-normal, three optimisation. The optimisation ones all have a constraint that must be used to remove a variable.

6.1Related rates

Differentiate the relation with respect to t, then substitute the instantaneous values. The rate asked for is the one on the left; everything else is data.

Needed forExemplar Ch 6 Q35, Q3 (the kite)

6.2A rate with respect to something other than time

FT Q14 asks for dVdh, not dVdt. Express V in terms of that one variable and differentiate once.

Watch outNo dhdt is given and none is needed — read which variable the question actually names.

Needed forFT Q14

6.3Increasing and decreasing from the sign of f ′

f ′ > 0 on an interval ⇒ strictly increasing there. Factorise f ′ and read the sign off the roots.

Watch outA positive-leading quadratic is negative between its roots; a negative-leading one is positive between them.

Needed forFT Q1(x), Exemplar Ch 6 Q46

6.4Tangent and normal

Slope of the tangent is dydx at the point; the normal's slope is −1 ÷ that. Then use y − y1 = m(x − x1).

Watch out−1m, not −m.

Needed forFT Q5, FT Q5(OR), Exemplar Ch 6 Q41, Q14

6.5‘The tangent is this particular line’

Matching the slope is necessary but not sufficient. Find every point with that slope, then check which of them actually lies on the given line.

Watch outIn FT Q5 two points have slope 1 and only one lies on y = x − 11.

Needed forFT Q5

6.6Implicit differentiation

Differentiate both sides as they stand, treating y as a function of x, then solve for dydx.

Watch out√x + √y = 4 gives dydx = −√y√x — always negative, which decides the sign when the tangent is ‘equally inclined’.

Needed forFT Q5(OR), Exemplar Ch 6 Q14

6.7Maxima and minima: the two tests

First derivative: f ′ changes + to − at a maximum. Second derivative: f ′ = 0 and f ″ < 0 gives a maximum.

Watch outIf f ″ is also zero the second test says nothing — fall back on the sign of f ′.

Needed forPB Q5(b), FT Q14(OR), Exemplar Ch 6 Q31

6.8On a closed interval, test the endpoints

The absolute maximum of a continuous function on [a, b] is the largest of the values at the stationary points and at a and b.

Watch outIn FT Q1(v) both stationary points lie outside [−1, 1], so the answer is at an endpoint and solving f ′ = 0 alone finds nothing.

Needed forFT Q1(v)

6.9Constrained optimisation

Use the constraint to eliminate one variable before differentiating, or differentiate the constraint to get drda and substitute.

Watch outTreating the two variables as independent and setting both derivatives to zero collapses the problem to nothing.

Needed forPB Q5(b), FT Q14(OR), Exemplar Ch 6 Q31, NCERT Ch 6

6.10Maximise the square when a root is in the way

A2 and A have their maximum at the same place when A > 0, and A2 has no square root in it.

Needed forExemplar Ch 6 (largest inscribed rectangle), ISC Specimen 2023

7. Integrals

Only three questions in the two papers — the 2024–25 paper has no integration at all — but all three are property-and-identity questions rather than routine ones.

7.1The half-angle identities

1 − cos x = 2 sin2 x2,   1 + cos x = 2 cos2 x2,   sin x = 2 sin x2 cos x2

Watch outThese turn an apparently hopeless quotient into cosec2 and cot, which integrate at sight.

Needed forPB Q1(e), NCERT Ch 7

7.2f ′(x)f(x) integrates to log|f(x)|

Before any substitution, check whether the numerator is the derivative of the denominator.

Needed forPB Q1(e), NCERT Ch 7

7.3Integration by parts, and the cancellation pattern

∫u dvdx dx = uv − ∫v dudx dx

Watch outIn PB Q1(e) the term parts produces is exactly the one already waiting to be integrated, and the two cancel. Look for that before grinding on.

Needed forPB Q1(e)

7.4The a − x reflection property

∫a0f(x) dx = ∫a0f(a − x) dx

Watch outOn [0, π2] it swaps sin and cos, and tan and cot. Adding the two forms usually gives 2I = something trivial.

Needed forPB Q1(f), Exemplar Ch 7 Q30

7.5Rewrite before you substitute

11 + tan x = cos xcos x + sin x is what exposes the symmetry; in that form the property works, in the original it does not.

Needed forPB Q1(f), NCERT Ch 7 Example 6

7.6Combining √tan x and √cot x

Put them over a common denominator to get sin x + cos x√sin x cos x, then use

Watch out2 sin x cos x = 1 − (sin x − cos x)2 — not sin 2x, which leaves a root you cannot substitute into.

Needed forPB Q6(a), Exemplar Ch 7 Q43

7.7Changing the limits with the variable

In a definite integral, either convert the limits when you substitute or convert back before evaluating. Never mix the two.

Needed forPB Q1(f)

8. Application of Integrals

One question in the two papers, but it needs the whole method — and the first step is not integration at all.

8.1Identify the curve first

y2 = 2x − x2 rearranges to (x − 1)2 + y2 = 1: a circle of radius 1 centred at (1, 0).

Watch outComplete the square before anything else. Integrating blindly turns a quarter circle into an intractable integral.

Needed forPB Q6(b), Exemplar Ch 8 Q16

8.2Area between two curves

A = ∫ba(yupper − ylower) dx

Watch outDecide which curve is on top by testing one convenient x between the intersections.

Needed forPB Q6(b), Exemplar Ch 8 Q16

8.3Split where the boundary changes

If the upper curve changes, or a modulus changes definition, the integral splits at that point.

Needed forExemplar Ch 8 Q18 (triangle), Q23 (modulus)

8.4Area is not the signed integral

Where the curve dips below the axis the integral is negative. An 'area' answer of zero always means a missing modulus.

Needed forExemplar Ch 8 Q17 (sin x over [0, 2π])

8.5Standard areas worth recognising

  • A quarter circle of radius a has area πa24.
  • A sector of angle θ has area 12r2θ.
  • Integrating along y is often shorter when both curves give x in terms of y.

Needed forPB Q6(b), Exemplar Ch 8 Q27, Q29

9. Linear Programming

Five questions — and the two long ones are the same diet problem, set eight years apart with the grains and prices exchanged. Formulation is most of the marks.

9.1Formulating

  1. Name the variables, with their units.
  2. One inequality per resource or requirement.
  3. Write the objective, and say whether it is to be maximised or minimised.
  4. Add x, y ≥ 0.

Watch outConvert the units first. Costs quoted per kg against contents quoted per gram is exactly where these questions catch people.

Needed forFT Q22, PB Q13(b), Exemplar Ch 12 Q23

9.2Half planes, strict and non-strict

A linear inequality is a half plane. A strict one (<) excludes its boundary; test the origin to see which side is meant.

Needed forFT Q19(ii), Exemplar Ch 12

9.3The corner point theorem

On a bounded feasible region the optimum of a linear objective is attained at a corner point. Evaluate Z at each and compare.

Needed forFT Q19(v), FT Q20, Exemplar Ch 12 Q1, Q10

9.4Unbounded regions need one more check

If the region is unbounded, a corner value M is the minimum only if the open half plane ax + by < M has no point in common with the region.

Watch outQuoting the smallest corner value without this check is an incomplete answer — a minimum need not exist at all.

Needed forFT Q22, PB Q13(b)

9.5Ties between two corners

If Z takes the same value at two corner points, the objective line is parallel to that edge and every point of the edge is optimal.

Needed forFT Q19(v), Exemplar Ch 12 Q22 (the bikes)

9.6Feasible solutions against the optimal solution

Every point of the region is a feasible solution — so there are usually infinitely many. Only the optimum is confined to the corners.

Watch outFT Q20 asks for the number of feasible solutions, not the maximum. The answer is ‘infinite’.

Needed forFT Q20

9.7Not every pair of lines gives a corner

Two constraint lines may meet outside the feasible region, and a constraint may be redundant. Test each intersection against all the constraints.

Needed forExemplar Ch 12 Q21
